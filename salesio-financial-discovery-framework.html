The Math Stage, Closer by Closer

What each one actually does with the numbers, why it works, and a script you can plug your own prospect's numbers into and use live.

Closer 1
Samuel
Use when: they have money, no real debt problem
What he does
Builds the necessities total out loud first, gets every debt's balance and its interest rate, then runs one real calculation live: takes their target monthly retirement spend, multiplies it by 300 (the 4% rule), and gets a hard number, $3.6M in his actual call. Then he shows their current savings rate as a percent (6.7%) next to a target (20%), and converts both into years, 19 vs. 14-15. Last, he prices out the alternative: what an advisor would cost them in fees over that same stretch, $666,000.
Why it works
Nobody feels a percentage. Everybody feels years of their life. "You're 5 years behind" lands where "you're saving 13% too little" doesn't. And pricing the alternative (advisor fees) before pricing anything he's selling makes doing nothing, or paying someone else, look like the expensive choice, not the safe one.
The script

"Let's figure out your actual number. If you're spending about [$X] a month once you're retired, we use something called the 4% rule, take that number and multiply it by 300. That gives you [$X × 300]. That's the real target."

"Right now, based on what you're putting away, you're saving about [X]% of your gross income. We usually want to see 20%. At your current rate, it's going to take you somewhere around [X] years to hit that number. If we get you to 20%, that drops to about [Y] years. So we're talking about [Z] fewer years you'd have to work."

"Now here's the other side of it. If nothing changes and you hand this off to an advisor to manage for you instead, over that same stretch of time you'd pay somewhere around [$X] in fees alone. That's the actual cost of not knowing how to do this yourself."

Closer 2
Rohail
Use when: money's fine, but partners can't agree or can't stick to a plan
What he does
Barely runs math at all, on purpose. He gets the basic numbers (income, retirement balance, debts) but instead of a live calculation, he stacks direct yes-or-no questions: "If nothing changes, are you able to get the house? Enough for retirement, where you both don't have to work? Enough for the kids? Yes or no?" Three no's, back to back, said out loud by them, not by him.
Why it works
When the real problem is that a couple can't agree, more numbers just give them something new to argue about. Forcing them to answer their own yes/no questions means they build the case for change themselves, in their own voice. That's much stickier than being told.
The script

"Let's say nothing changes from how you're doing things right now, and [X] years go by. Are you able to get [the house / their stated goal]? [wait for the answer]"

"Do you have enough set up that both of you don't have to work anymore? [wait]"

"Is there enough set aside so [the kids / their stated priority] is actually taken care of? [wait]"

"So right now, on the path we're on: no. No. No. Okay. So we need to make a change."

Closer 3
Imran
Use when: retired or no active income, but they have real assets
What he does
Pulls up the prospect's actual account live and walks them through finding their own numbers (gain/loss, dividend income) instead of just asking. Then he runs one simple comparison: annual income the portfolio is generating, against how big the portfolio actually is. "You're making $55K a year on an $800,000 portfolio. For that size, that's not good at all. We should be doing a lot more."
Why it works
Doing the discovery inside their real account, not hypothetically, means they're already getting value before any pitch happens, which builds trust fast. And the yield comparison reframes the whole conversation: it's not "how much do you have," it's "how hard is it actually working." For someone who isn't earning anymore, that's the only lever left.
The script

"Let's pull up your actual account together, go to [platform].com. Can you find the gain/loss number for this year?"

"Okay, so based on that, you're making about [$X] a year off a [$Y] portfolio."

"For a portfolio that size, that's not good at all, we should be doing a lot more than that. Your money should be doing the work here, and right now it isn't."

Closer 4
Kyle
Use when: good income, but debt and expenses are the real concern
What he does
Splits the conversation into two hard categories and never lets them blend. First, necessities only, mortgage, groceries, insurance, phone, with debt payments explicitly excluded, narrated as a running total until it's a real number. Second, every single debt, and when the prospect answers with a monthly payment instead of a balance, he stops them immediately: "No, the total of the balances."
Why it works
Blending debt payments into "expenses" hides how much surplus actually exists to attack the real problem. And a monthly payment can feel manageable while masking a genuinely large balance, insisting on the total balance every time is the only way the prospect sees how big the problem really is.
The script

"Let's split this into two things: what you truly need to live on, and what you owe. First, necessities only, no debt payments in this number. What's your mortgage or rent? ... Utilities? ... Groceries? [running total after each one] Okay, so that's about [$X] a month just to live."

"Now let's go through the debt side. When I ask this, I don't mean the monthly payment, I mean the total balance you owe. Credit cards, what's the total? Not the minimum payment, the total."

"Any personal loans, what's the total balance? Student loans, total?"
