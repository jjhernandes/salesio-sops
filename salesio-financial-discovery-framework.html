Financial Discovery Framework

Built by reverse-engineering four real financial coaching sales calls, four different closers, same company (automatedrev.com — the "Finance to Freedom" / BudgetDog program). Same underlying offer structure every time, four completely different styles of getting a prospect there.

The four calls:
• Samuel Leeland × Brooke Bekkedahl — W-2 couple, wealth optimization. ~42 of 120 min. Full numbers + goals, cuts off before the pitch.
• Rohail Akhtar × John & Lindsey Frank — couple in open conflict over spending. Reaches the transition into program structure before cutting off.
• Imran Ahmed × Susan Ballazhi — retiree, dividend income. Goes deepest into the actual pitch mechanics before cutting off right before the price reveal.
• Kyle Padilla × Brent & Dawn Bomer — 30-year debt cycle. ~50 of 122 min. Richest psychology of the four, cuts off right as it transitions toward the ask.
None of the four fully reveal price or objection handling, so that part's still not here. Everything else, the numbers technique, the psychology, and now the actual offer structure, is built only from what's in these four transcripts.
1

The Numbers Technique

None of the four closers just ask "what do you make and what do you owe." Every one of them builds the full picture live with the prospect, then uses at least one real calculation to turn a vague "are we okay?" into a hard, personal number.
Tactic 1
Disarm the sales frame before a single number is asked for
Before anything financial comes up, Samuel Leeland states his objective plainly: "Frankly, we don't need another client... if what you have right now is perfect, God bless, I can lay my head on the pillow." Every number Brooke gives afterward is given to someone positioned as an evaluator of her situation, not a seller of a program.
Tactic 2
Announce the segments out loud, one at a time
Leeland: "Let's go segment by segment — first cash flow, then investments, then debt management." The prospect always knows what's coming next, which lowers resistance and stops the conversation from feeling like an interrogation.
Tactic 3
Set the target structure before pulling the actuals
Before asking what hits the account monthly, Leeland asks what the ideal structure would be — live off base salary, treat commission as upside. That answer becomes the frame every actual number gets measured against, instead of numbers just sitting there uninterpreted.
Tactic 4
Build the running total live, line by line
Mortgage → loans → childcare → utilities → groceries → insurance → phone — each its own question, narrated as a running subtotal, in every single one of the four calls. Critical-core expenses get isolated from discretionary spend without judgment attached to either bucket. Kyle Padilla draws the hardest line of the four: "Just necessities before any of the debt payback, the minimums there."
Leeland/Bekkedahl: "$11,100 in critical core expenses" vs. "another $1,000 to $1,300" discretionary, named separately. Padilla/Bomer: necessities land at $9,100, rounded to $10K, with debt service broken out after.
Tactic 5
Never accept a payment when you asked for a balance — or a balance without a rate
When Brent starts answering with a monthly payment, Padilla stops him cold: "No, the total of the balances." Payment tells you cash flow; balance tells you the real size of the problem. Leeland runs the inverse discipline on Bekkedahl's debts — every balance also gets a rate, because the entire pay-down-vs-invest call later depends on it. A 0% loan, a 4% loan, and a 3.5% mortgage all get treated completely differently once the rate is known.
Tactic 6
Turn live discovery into a teaching moment, not just an audit
Imran Ahmed walks Susan through her own Schwab account in real time, teaching her where to find her portfolio's gain/loss data as they go. She's learning something during the interrogation, not just being interrogated, which builds reciprocity before any pitch happens. Fifteen minutes later she's the one volunteering exact dividend figures unprompted.
Tactic 7
Normalize with a real comparison before any diagnosis
Right after hearing Brooke's numbers, Leeland drops in an anonymized reference point: "I had a conversation with a couple yesterday, $1.3 million in consumer debt... their mortgage is 50% of their income." Her situation instantly reads as strong instead of alarming, which keeps her open instead of defensive for the numbers still to come.
Tactic 8
Calculate the actual finish line, live, in front of them
Leeland runs the 4% rule out loud with Bekkedahl's own target spend: $12,000/month × 300 = $3.6M. Ahmed does the equivalent with Susan's existing $820K portfolio against her $55K/year income: "For an $800,000 portfolio, that's not good at all. We should be doing a lot more." Either way, "are we on track" stops being a feeling and becomes one number they now own.
Tactic 9
Quantify the cost of the default option — the "devil's number"
Leeland shows Bekkedahl's savings rate (6.7% vs. a recommended 20%) as both a percentage gap and a time gap — 19 years vs. 14–15 years to the number — then quantifies what handing that money to an advisor costs over the same span: "$666,000 in fees." Ahmed runs the same play with Susan more simply: her old advisor charged roughly 2% a year, and once she's educated, "your level is that of an advisor... you'll never need it." Doing nothing, or staying with the default option, gets reframed as the expensive choice, not the safe one.
Tactic 10
Stack forced yes/no questions until the gap is undeniable
Rohail Akhtar doesn't editorialize the Franks' numbers — he interrogates them with it: "Are you able to get the house? Enough for retirement, where you both don't have to work? Enough for the kids? Yes or no?" Three "no's" in a row, stacked back to back, do more work than any single dramatic number could. The prospect builds their own case for change out loud.
⚠️
Why this matters for a $7,500 sale: the same discipline — real net income split from headline income, critical-core isolated from discretionary spend, every debt tied to a rate not just a balance — is exactly what determines whether someone can do pay-in-full, a split, or needs financing. Getting it wrong either kills a real deal or sets up a payment plan that collapses in month two.
2

The Psychology Layer

The numbers build the case. These are the moves, across all four closers, that make the prospect feel the case in their own words before any pitch happens.
Tactic 11
Handle an absent or reluctant decision-maker without losing a beat
When Brooke explains her husband can't make it and that she's the financial decision-maker, Leeland doesn't probe or push back — "Your choice, whatever. Fair enough. It doesn't change too much for me." Instant reassignment of authority, zero friction, momentum never breaks.
Tactic 12
Open with real rapport, not a scripted icebreaker
Leeland and Brooke trade genuine Mariners/Blue Jays banter. Imran and Susan spend two full minutes on whether his background is a real window or a screen. Neither is a template line — it's what buys the trust needed for a stranger to hand over exact account balances twenty minutes later.
Tactic 13
Use the couple's live disagreement as diagnostic material
Instead of smoothing over tension between John and Lindsey Frank, Rohail Akhtar leans into it, asking each of them directly whether they agree with what the other just said. They end up arguing the real problem out loud, in front of him, without him ever having to state it himself.
Tactic 14
Ask for the pattern before the point-in-time number
Before any dollar figure, Kyle Padilla asks the Bomers "how long has that been going on?" Learning this is a 30-year cycle, not a one-time setback, raises the emotional stakes far more than any single number could.
Tactic 15
Reflect their own soft language back at them
When Dawn says she "makes it work," Padilla doesn't let it pass — he names what it actually means: "Making it work... mostly means just surviving." This uses the prospect's own words to gently dismantle their own excuse, instead of arguing with them.
Tactic 16
Let them name the root cause — never say it first
"What do you think has been missing?" Brent arrives at "discipline" himself. Padilla never introduces the word. A diagnosis someone gives themselves is one they'll defend; a diagnosis you hand them is one they'll argue with.
Tactic 17
Tailor the self-pitching analogy to the specific prospect in front of you
Same underlying move, three different builds. Padilla's "friend who's 300 pounds and needs to lose weight" gets Brent and Dawn to say "get a trainer" and "accountability" unprompted. Akhtar adapts it to a diet-plan-without-mentality version aimed squarely at Lindsey, who cares about health. Ahmed skips the analogy entirely and tells Susan a real founder-origin story instead. None of them ever pitch the offer directly — the prospect describes the solution before the closer says a word about the program.
Tactic 18
Name the emotion directly, not just the behavior
Imran Ahmed doesn't stay in dollars and cents with Susan — he asks "Where does the guilt really come from?" She reveals she feels guilty for mismanaging inherited money. Naming the emotion out loud reaches somewhere the numbers alone never would.
Tactic 19
Build life-context and future-pace the vision before circling back to the pitch
Leeland gets Brooke's kids' ages and whether she should keep working before running a single retirement number. Ahmed asks Susan directly: "What do you want your days to actually look like?" By the time the numbers get run, they're being run against a life the prospect already described wanting, not a generic target.
Tactic 20
Reflect their stated identity back as the reason the plan fits them
Brooke volunteers that she's "a bit of a control freak" who wants to understand things herself, not hand off blind trust to an advisor. Leeland's entire pitch angle — self-sufficiency, "that advisor becomes obsolete" — is the exact mirror of what she said she wants. He never has to argue for it; it's already her own stated preference.

The Framework — 12 Stages

Generalized from all four calls. Use this as a numbers-discovery layer inside your existing call — it slots naturally into Current Reality and Problem Awareness on the standard Sales Call Script. Stages 11 and 12 go further than the earlier version of this doc could, because two of the four calls actually reach the transition into the offer.
1
Disarm the Frame Before Any Numbers
Say plainly you're not there to upsell — before a single figure is asked for

State directly that if what they have is already working, you'll tell them so, and that's a fine outcome. People give real numbers when they believe they're being helped, not evaluated for a sale. This is Leeland's opening move with Brooke, word for word.

💡
Say something close to: "My objective is to figure out where you're actually at — regardless of whether we end up working together. If what you have is perfect, great, I'll tell you that."
2
Get the Open-Ended Story First
Ask what's hardest before opening a single spreadsheet

"What's the biggest challenge you're facing right now?" lets them self-report the real pain point unprompted, in their own words, before any number anchors or narrows the conversation. Every one of the four calls opens this way, whether it's Brooke's "we don't really know what we're doing," Susan's "I have to juggle and double check," or the Franks arguing about trips versus the house before a single dollar figure comes up.

3
Set the Target Structure Before the Actuals
Define what "on track" means before doing any math

Ask what the ideal setup would look like — e.g. living off base income with variable comp as pure upside — before pulling real monthly figures. Every actual number that follows now has something to be measured against.

4
Build Cash Flow Line by Line, Out Loud
Narrate a running total; separate critical-core from discretionary

Announce the segment ("let's talk cash flow first, then investments, then debt"), then go housing, insurance, childcare, groceries, transportation — one at a time, restating the running total after each answer. Keep discretionary spend (dining, subscriptions, shopping) in its own separate bucket, without judgment attached to either number. With Susan, Imran Ahmed goes one step further and turns this into a live walkthrough of her actual brokerage account, so she's learning her own numbers alongside him instead of just reciting them.

💡
This is what reveals real surplus — the actual capacity available before anything gets pitched.
5
Get Every Debt's Balance AND Rate
Never accept a payment when you asked for a balance, or a balance without a rate

Mortgage, loans, credit cards — each gets both numbers. The rate is what the entire later pay-down-vs-invest recommendation hinges on. Kyle Padilla stops Brent mid-answer to correct exactly this: "No, the total of the balances," not the monthly payment. Skip either half and you can't actually answer their real question.

6
Ask for the Pattern, Not Just the Snapshot
"How long has this been going on?" before any number lands

Padilla asks the Bomers how long they've been cycling in and out of debt. "Our whole marriage, 30-plus years" reframes everything that follows — this isn't a bad quarter, it's a lifelong pattern, and that raises the emotional stakes before a single dollar figure does.

7
Let Them Name the Root Cause, Then Reflect Their Own Language Back
Ask, don't tell — then use their own words against their own excuse

"What do you think has actually gotten in the way?" A self-given diagnosis gets defended; a diagnosis you hand them gets argued with. Brent arrives at "discipline" on his own. When Dawn later says she "makes it work," Padilla doesn't let the phrase pass unexamined — he names what it actually means: "making it work mostly means just surviving." Imran runs a deeper version of the same move with Susan, asking directly "where does the guilt really come from," rather than staying in dollars and cents.

8
Use an Analogy Tailored to This Prospect to Let Them Pitch Themselves
Get them to describe the solution before you introduce it

Find an analogy adjacent to their actual problem — physical health, a skill they already respect experts in — and ask what that person should do. Padilla's "friend who's 300 pounds and needs to lose weight" gets Brent and Dawn to say "get a trainer" and "accountability" unprompted, then he just draws the parallel. Not every closer uses the same vehicle: Ahmed skips the analogy with Susan and tells a real founder-origin story instead. Match the device to the person, not a fixed script.

9
Calculate the Actual Finish Line, Live
Turn "are we okay?" into one concrete number, in front of them

Use a real formula (the 4% rule, or equivalent) with their own target numbers to produce one hard figure. Leeland does this with Bekkedahl's $12K/month target ($3.6M). Ahtar stacks it as forced yes/no questions with the Franks: "are you able to get the house, retirement for both of you, enough for the kids? Yes or no?" — three no's in a row that they say out loud, not that he tells them. Either approach turns vague anxiety into a specific, ownable target.

10
Quantify the Cost of the Default Option
Make inaction, or the passive alternative, sound expensive

Calculate what doing nothing, or handing the problem to an advisor, actually costs over the same timeframe, in real dollars. Leeland's version is $666,000 in advisor fees over 19 years. Ahmed's is simpler but does the same job: once Susan is educated, "your level is that of an advisor... you'll never need it." This reframes "pay us" against "pay someone else, or pay in lost time," not against "pay nothing."

11
Stack Their Own Gaps Into the Case for Help
List back — in their language — every unaddressed problem surfaced during discovery

Right before transitioning to the pitch, name each gap that came up naturally during the call: no tax strategy, no will, no plan for the kids, idle cash sitting uninvested. Individually minor; said together as a list, right before the ask, they compound into a case the prospect essentially built themselves. Leeland does this explicitly with Brooke, and it's the exact moment both Akhtar and Ahmed also pivot into describing the program.

12
Present the Offer as Three Pillars: Track, Grow, Protect
The one piece of structure that's identical across every closer

This is the most useful finding from having all four calls side by side: the individual style is completely different, confrontational versus warm versus numbers-driven versus relational, but the underlying offer is presented the exact same way every time. Leeland names it early with Brooke: "it's essentially the three pillars of exactly what we do... track your money, grow your money, protect it." Ahmed spells out the identical structure to Susan almost verbatim: step one is track your money, step two is implementation of the investment plan, step three is protecting it, meaning tax strategy. Whatever variation exists is in how a closer gets a prospect there, not in what the company is actually selling.

Ahmed's call also surfaces infrastructure the other three never mention: a mandatory one-on-one with a "financial therapist" before the main process starts, weekly accountability check-ins, and an explicit good-cop-closer, bad-cop-coach dynamic once someone's enrolled ("Brent's a nice guy, but when it comes to client results, he can turn into a completely different person").

💡
None of the four transcripts reach an explicit price reveal or objection handling, so that part still isn't here. From this point, the natural next steps mirror the existing Sales Call Script: Price Reveal → Objection Handling → Close. Send a transcript that reaches that far and this framework gets finished properly.
4

Matching the Math to the Problem

The math a closer runs live isn't one fixed formula. Every one of the four calls has a completely different prospect problem, and the math changes entirely depending on which problem it actually is. Running the wrong math on the right person kills the call.
Brooke Bekkedahl (Leeland) — Surplus, under-optimized
Dual W-2 income, positive cash flow, $113K sitting idle, no debt problem. The constraint is allocation efficiency, not income or spending. Math used: the 4% rule to get a hard retirement number ($3.6M), then savings-rate converted straight into years (19 vs. 14-15), plus fee-avoidance math ($666K). This is compounding-acceleration math. It only works because there's already surplus to redirect.
John & Lindsey Frank (Akhtar) — Income is fine, alignment isn't
High household income, no real debt crisis, $860K combined retirement. The constraint is behavioral: they've never agreed on a number, and one spouse keeps overriding the other's attempts to cut spending. Math used: almost none, on purpose. Akhtar never runs a live calculation. He stacks forced yes/no questions until three "no's" in a row build the case, then defers the actual number-crunching to the program itself. Running complex math here would've just given them something new to argue about.
Susan Ballazhi (Ahmed) — Retired, asset-rich, yield-poor
No earned income, $820K portfolio, house paid off, living entirely off ~$55K/year in dividends and Social Security. She isn't accumulating toward a future number, she's already decumulating. Math used: yield-adequacy critique, not a time-horizon calc — "you're making $55K on an $800K portfolio, we should be doing a lot more" — plus tax-drag quantification ($74K anticipated tax bill). The lever is restructuring what already exists, because nothing more is coming in.
Brent & Dawn Bomer (Padilla) — Genuine debt crisis
~$25K/month revenue but $417K+ in non-mortgage debt across credit cards, student loans, a personal loan, and a car note, cycling for 30 years, denied for consolidation. Math used: necessities-first triage — true necessities total with debt explicitly excluded, then every debt inventoried by total balance, not payment, by category. No retirement or investment math at all. That's not on the table until the debt is under control.

💡
The real skill isn't knowing the 4% rule or knowing how to build a budget. It's diagnosing which pillar is actually broken before deciding which math to run. Retirement-number math on the Bomers would've been tone-deaf. Necessities-triage on Bekkedahl would've been irrelevant — she doesn't have that problem.
5

The Quick Diagnostic

Generalized, simple, usable live on a call. Ask these in the first few minutes of discovery, before you run any numbers. The answers tell you which of four profiles you're in — and that decides everything about which math to run next.
1
Are they currently earning income, or retired / living off fixed income?
2
Is debt a real burden — high balances, missed payments, denied for consolidation — or manageable/paid off monthly?
3
If there's a spouse or partner involved, do they already agree on the goal, or is there open disagreement about money?
1. Optimization
Earning, debt is manageable, aligned with partner (or single)
Run
Retirement-number math: 4% rule on target monthly spend, current savings rate converted into years, fee-cost of the default option.
Skip
Debt triage, yield critique — neither applies here.
2. Alignment
Earning, debt is manageable, but partners disagree or can't stick to a plan together
Run
Forced yes/no questions to build consensus out loud. Get both partners agreeing the current path fails before touching a calculator.
Skip
Live retirement math — defer it. More numbers just becomes more to disagree about.
3. Yield
Retired or no active income, has existing assets, no earned income coming in
Run
Yield-adequacy critique on the existing portfolio, plus tax-drag cost. "Is this pile working hard enough?" not "how many years until freedom."
Skip
Time-horizon / years-to-retirement math — there's no future accumulation phase left to project.
4. Triage
Debt is a real burden regardless of income level — high balances, missed payments, denied credit
Run
Necessities-first budget (debt excluded), then every debt by total balance and rate, by category.
Skip
Any investment, retirement, or yield conversation. Not relevant until the debt is handled.
💡
If more than one profile seems to fit, go with whichever pillar is the most urgent constraint. A prospect with both debt and misalignment is still Profile 4 first — debt triage always outranks alignment work, because there's no plan to align around until the bleeding stops.
